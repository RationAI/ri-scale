4. MMCI: models from the Excel sheet

Ridge Cox models with median imputation and dummy coding; missing categories form their own "unknown" level and levels with fewer than 10 patients are merged. Five-fold cross-validation on 176 training patients, then a one-time evaluation on a held-out test set of 44 patients (18 deaths).

5. MUG: a large cohort with a long history

The shared-feature set is what both institutes record: age, sex, pT, pN, M and grade. Because MUG spans 30 years, the choice of period matters more than the choice of stage range.

What worked: the lymph-node ratio approach. A node detector is trained with only "does the patient have a positive node" as a label; a patient is positive when any node is. Its expected number of positive nodes, their share of all nodes, the highest node score and the node count then enter a Cox model with age and sex. It beats age + sex by +0.033 to +0.051 C-index and by +0.06 to +0.08 AUC at five years, in every repeat. Averaging all node embeddings (+0.005) or an attention model (-0.007) did not.

Where it falls short: the staging reference reaches 0.74, and images with staging (0.72) are slightly below staging alone. The images capture nodal burden but not metastasis (M1) or the depth of the primary tumour, which carry most of the signal in this cohort.

Do the embeddings see the staging?

mean pooling of node embeddingsmax pooling

Cross-validated AUC for predicting staging labels from the pooled embeddings (0.5 is chance). Node metastasis is detected very well (0.92), pT well, and M1 only moderately. This explains why the ratio approach works and why the images cannot reach the staging model on their own.

Tile-level version. A detector that scores every tile (4.0 million core tiles of the 214 patients, reduced to 128 dimensions) finds patients with positive nodes better than the node-level one (AUC 0.97 vs 0.91) and its ratio matches the pathologist's more closely (Spearman 0.69 vs 0.59 among positive patients). The survival gain, however, stays the same: +0.037 to +0.044 C-index, against +0.051 for the best node-level model, all within the noise (the spread of the repeats is 0.006-0.014). Better detection of the nodes does not add survival information beyond the node-level ratio, which suggests the ratio itself is worth about +0.04 to +0.05 here.

7. Lymph-node segmentation (SAM3)

The fine-tuned SAM3 mask decoder was trained on MMCI slides and then tested on MUG, a different scanner and stain. It generalised in quality but missed small nodes. Retraining on MMCI plus MUG (476 MUG training slides, 135 held-out MUG test slides) fixed most of the gap.

8. Limits and next steps

  • Small sample. 87 deaths in MMCI means the C-index has an uncertainty of about 0.03. Gains below 0.05 are hard to prove; the repeated folds help, but they are not independent.
  • Image ratio vs the pathologist's ratio. The node-level detector finds patients with positive nodes well (AUC 0.91) and the tile-level one better (0.97), but the correlation of the ratio with the pathologist's counts is moderate (Spearman 0.59 and 0.69 among positive patients). Serial sections count the same node more than once.
  • Training labels. The ratio models use the pathologist's node counts to train the detector. They are never inputs, but a method that avoids them entirely would be weaker.
  • MUG images are not linked to MUG patients (the CSV has only a pseudonymous patient label). A mapping would give about 20 times more patients for the image models.
  • Next: the primary-tumour slides, which carry pT and invasion information the lymph nodes do not (the node ratio seems to be worth about +0.05, and the gap to staging is +0.12), and external data such as TCGA colorectal.
